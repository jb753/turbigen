Blade shapes¶

Camber, thickness, and how blade sections stack into a row.

A BladeDesign is the blade row specification as it appears in the input file under blades:, but is not in itself a shape: it defines recamber angles measured from a flow that is not known until the mean line is built. Combining a BladeDesign with a MeanLine and a RowAnnulus produces a Row with a full blade shape, a blade count, and a tip gap.

The Tutorial builds a blade in a complete design; this page documents the classes in more detail. The mean line a blade turns against is documented at Mean-line flow field and the annulus it sits in at Annulus lines.

Built-in shapes¶

A SectionDesign names a span fraction, a recamber at each end, and a camber and a thickness design. A BladeDesign holds one or more sections in increasing span fraction, together with a blade count, a tip clearance, a swirl distribution and a stacking position. Each of these is again a design paired with a result, the same split the rest of turbigen makes at machine level:

Quadratic and Bernstein are the built-in camber shapes and Taylor the built-in thickness distribution; all are documented in the sections below.

The number of blades comes from a BladeCount rule on the design:

  • FixedCount (Nb) states it directly;

  • Circulation (Co) sets it from a circulation coefficient and the surface length;

  • DiffusionFactor (DFL) sets it from the Lieblein diffusion factor and the chord.

A blade has one tip clearance, stated as a fraction of span (tip_span), a fraction of meridional chord (tip_chord) or an absolute length (tip_metre); giving more than one is an error.

Design process¶

turbigen converts each entry of blades: into a row BladeDesign and, once the mean line and annulus are built, passes them design() which:

  1. Calculates leading and trailing edge metal angles for each section as the local relative flow angle plus the recambers dchi_LE and dchi_TE. The flow angle is read from the mean-line row, with the swirl varied radially using the vortex_exponent onto the section span fraction;

  2. Camber and thickness designs, the metal angles, the stacking position and the blade rotation are collected across all sections into a Blade for each row;

  3. The BladeCount rule reads the finished Blade to fix the number of blades.

Evaluating a blade¶

A Blade is addressed by span fraction, and every geometric quantity is read off it with an evaluate_ method that interpolates the section designs field by field, extrapolating beyond the end sections. evaluate_section() gives the axial, radial and angular coordinates of the two surfaces; evaluate_chi() the leading and trailing metal angles; and evaluate_chord() and evaluate_surface_length() the meridional chord and the longer surface length used by the count rules.

A number of blades installed in an annulus.

Separate from the shape it holds, because the two are independent: how a blade is shaped says nothing about how many of them there are, and every consumer wants one or the other, never both. Camber lines¶

A CamberDesign describes the shape of a camber line between its end angles; pairing it with those angles gives a CamberLine, which can be evaluated. The end angles are not design variables but a result — the local flow angle plus the recamber asked for by a SectionDesign — so the split here is the same one made everywhere else, one level down.

Quadratic (quadratic) is the built-in shape: the camber line slope varies quadratically along the chord, with aft_loading shifting the turning towards the trailing edge.

Bernstein (bernstein) is the flexible alternative: a linear angle-tangent ramp plus order - 1 interior Bernstein coefficients that perturb it. The endpoint coefficients are pinned at zero so the ends stay put, and all-zero coefficients recover Quadratic with zero aft loading.

Thickness distributions¶

A ThicknessDesign evaluates thickness as a function of normalised meridional distance. Thickness is always normalised by the meridional chord, so there is no reference length to choose: a design is evaluable as soon as it exists, rather than only once a chord is known.

Taylor (taylor) is the built-in distribution: two cubic splines in shape space meeting at the point of maximum thickness, after [Taylor and Miller, 2016].

peak()¶

Return the largest half-thickness anywhere in 0 <= m <= 1 [–].

Exact rather than sampled. The square root in thick() is what makes it awkward, and substituting m = u**2 removes it: for a cubic tau = a m^3 + b m^2 + c m + d the half-thickness becomes a polynomial in u,

t(u) = -a u^9 + (a-b) u^7 + (b-c) u^5 + (c-d) u^3

  • (t_TE/2) u^2 + d u

so the stationary points are the real roots of a degree-8 derivative and the maximum is the largest of t at those and at the ends of the interval. Sampling instead would need a point count chosen by taste: a coarse grid misses shallow excursions, and there is no density that is obviously enough.

The ends are always included, so the peak location can never be missed outright; and because m_tmax is an end of both pieces, t_max itself is always a candidate. The comparison is therefore always “does it exceed its declared peak”, never “did we happen to find it”.

tau(m)¶

Return thickness in shape space at normalised meridional distance m.

thick(m)¶

Return half-thickness at normalised meridional distance m.

The trailing edge thickness is specified as the total due to both sides, so half of it is returned at the trailing edge.
